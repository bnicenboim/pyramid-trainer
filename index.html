Pyramid Trainer

The pyramid exercise builds rhythmic precision by cycling through subdivisions in sequence:
Quarter → Eighth → Triplet → Sixteenth → Triplet → Eighth → Quarter

The metronome clicks on quarter notes only. You subdivide internally and count aloud:
  • Quarters: count "1 2 3 4"
  • Eighths: "1 + 2 + 3 + 4 +" — say one and two and three and four and (the "+" is pronounced "and")
  • Triplets: "1 trip let 2 trip let…" — say one-trip-let two-trip-let, three even syllables per beat
  • Sixteenths: "1 e + a 2 e + a…" — say one-ee-and-uh two-ee-and-uh, four even syllables per beat
Start slowly (40–60 BPM). Count aloud for one pass before picking. With Record on, the session is recorded; afterwards the waveform of your guitar is shown against the subdivision grid, and you can listen back to hear where each note landed. Play the click through your speakers and keep the microphone on: the mic records the click next to your guitar, and the count-in clicks (heard with no guitar yet) anchor the grid of the recording exactly, so there is nothing to calibrate. With headphones, or with a direct input such as an audio interface, the input never hears the click: the app tells you right after the count-in, and places the grid from the delay the audio system reports instead (usually within ~10 ms, so spreads, drift and position comparisons are still meaningful, but a lean of a few ms is not). If you use an interface and want an exact grid, put a microphone on the input and the click on speakers for the session. Use Auto pyramid mode to cycle through the full sequence automatically.

Privacy: the recording, the takes history and the CSV log stay in your browser. Nothing is uploaded.

Where the timing bands, difficulty and lean thresholds come from (research notes)

This summary was written by an AI assistant (Claude) from the sources listed below. The two review papers were read directly; the other works are cited through them. Check the originals before relying on a specific number.

What a listener can hear

A single note displaced in a steady sequence becomes audible at about 6 ms when the notes are less than 240 ms apart, and at about 2.5% of the interval for 240–1,000 ms spacing (Friberg & Sundberg 1995). At 500 ms between notes that is 12–13 ms; at 1,200 ms, 30 ms. Those are laboratory thresholds for attentive listeners. In real music the tolerance is wider: notes meant to sound together in an ensemble typically fall 30–50 ms apart and are still heard as one attack (Rasch 1979).

How precisely trained players synchronize

Professional percussionists playing with a metronome scatter by about 16 ms (1.6%) at 1,000 ms between notes, about 10 ms (2%) at 500 ms, and close to 4% at 300 ms, where the rate becomes a biomechanical limit (Fujii et al. 2011). Drummers' interval variability is about 2.5% of an 800 ms beat (Krause et al. 2010). A fixed 25 ms window is therefore roughly a professional's 95% band. Players also sit systematically early without noticing: tapping, nonmusicians land about 50 ms ahead of the click and drummers about 20 ms (Krause et al. 2010); musicians playing their own instrument are only about 2 ms early, against 13 ms when tapping (Stoklasa et al. 2012).

Comfortable and extreme tempi

Timing is easiest and least variable for intervals of roughly 250–700 ms between sounded notes (Fraisse 1982; Repp 2005; London 2012). What matters is the interval between the notes actually played, not the quarter-note bpm. Subdividing a beat stops helping once the parts are shorter than 200–250 ms (Repp 2008); synchronizing becomes subjectively hard beyond about 1.8 s between beats (Bååth & Madison 2012), although predictive players keep going up to 3.5 s (Repp & Doggett 2007). Slow beats are made easier by subdividing them in one's head.

How this app uses the numbers

Green = within max(15 ms, 3% of the interval), capped at 25 ms: about twice the just-noticeable displacement. Amber = within max(35 ms, 7%), capped at 60 ms: inside the range where ensemble notes still sound together. The score counts amber or better. Difficulty weight: ×1.0 for 250–700 ms intervals, +0.35 per halving below 250 ms, +0.25 per doubling above 700 ms; length factor +0.15 per doubling of scored notes from 16, capped at ×1.6. A lean is reported from 15 ms, a spread from 35 ms. The weights are a reasoned mapping of this evidence, not values taken from a paper; the thresholds come from tapping and percussion studies, not guitar.

How Score and Points are calculated

For every grid line of the exercise (count-in bars excluded, and the beats after your last note excluded), the app looks for the strongest attack within half the interval around it and measures the deviation in ms. Score = number of beats whose deviation is inside the amber band ÷ number of beats, × 100; a beat with no attack found counts as a miss (0). It is plain accuracy, with no weighting, so two takes with the same score were played equally precisely.

Points = Score × difficulty, rounded. Difficulty = (average interval weight of the scored beats) × (length factor). The interval weight of a beat with interval I (ms to the next grid line) is 1 for 250 ≤ I ≤ 700; 1 + 0.35 × log₂(250 / I) below 250; 1 + 0.25 × log₂(I / 700) above 700. The length factor is 1 + 0.15 × log₂(N / 16) for N scored beats, clamped to 0.85–1.6. Example: 28 beats, a mix of quarters (1,200 ms, weight 1.19), eighths (600 ms, 1.0) and sixteenths (300 ms, 1.0) averaging 1.20, length factor 1 + 0.15 × log₂(28/16) = 1.12, difficulty 1.35; a score of 89 gives 89 × 1.35 ≈ 120 points. Points can exceed 100 on a hard take.
